The Dispatch

The US now licenses cloud GPUs abroad, and renting compute to China gets 90 days to stop

A new Commerce rule requires a licence for any data center, anywhere, that rents large amounts of US AI compute to Chinese-headquartered companies, and adds five countries to the chip licence list. Here is how the test works, who it hits, and what changes for people who rent or sell GPU time.

IndustryNovember 17, 20265 min read

The US Commerce Department on Monday closed the gap that let Chinese AI companies rent the Nvidia chips they are not allowed to buy. Under an interim final rule released on 16 November, any data center in the world needs a US licence before it supplies large amounts of advanced US compute to a company headquartered in China, Macau or another embargoed country.

That matters well beyond Beijing. Every GPU cloud, reseller and colocation operator outside the US now has to know who its customers really are, and anyone shipping top-end chips to Brazil, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore or Thailand needs a licence too.

The loophole everyone knew about

Since 2022 the US has blocked sales of its best AI chips to China, and since 2023 a licence has been required when the buyer is a company headquartered there, wherever it sets up shop. What the rules did not clearly stop was renting. A Chinese lab could sign a contract with a data center in Johor or Singapore, log in, and train on H100s it could never legally own.

Commerce had been urged to act for months. The pressure peaked after Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia's chief executive last week, following reports that linked him to an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore company that bought large numbers of Nvidia chips and is under US investigation over alleged smuggling. Nextgov reports that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent publicly faulted Commerce on Friday for not moving against Megaspeed, and Senator Maria Cantwell sent Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick a letter demanding action.

The BIS press release says the rule was written by and at the direction of Treasury and the National Security Council. Commerce departments do not usually announce that someone else drafted their regulations, and the line reads like a signal about who now sets chip policy.

How the new test works

The heart of the rule is a new section, 744.24, of the export regulations. It applies to chips and servers in the two classes that cover data center AI accelerators, 3A090 and 4A090. A licence is needed when the exporter, or the data center that later moves the hardware around, knows the gear will be used to give a company headquartered in China, Macau or a D:5 country (the US list of arms-embargoed nations, which includes Russia and Iran) access to more than 12,000,000 "aggregate TPP."

TPP, or total processing performance, is the export control yardstick for raw compute. One H100 scores a little under 16,000, so the line sits at roughly 750 of them. That is a mid-sized training cluster, not a hobbyist rig, but it is well within reach of the operators that rent to Chinese labs.

Three details make the test tougher than it first looks:

  • Linked sites count together. Splitting capacity across several data centers does not help if they share networking and workloads, and the rule says digital links count, not only physical ones.
  • Middlemen are covered. Renting to a Singapore firm that is training models for a Chinese parent or customer is treated as renting to the Chinese company.
  • It follows the hardware. A data center that starts serving a covered customer after the chips arrive needs a licence then, even if the original shipment was clean.

There is one carve-out. Compute used only for a "commercial recommender system," the ranking engines behind shopping and social feeds, is exempt if the operator reports to BIS. The definition is narrow on purpose: anything that generates text, images, code or other content does not qualify, even if it also recommends things.

Five new countries on the licence list

The second big change is geographic. Shipments of advanced AI chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil now need a licence, because Commerce says these places carry a higher-than-normal risk of diversion. Applications will be judged case by case, partly on how well each country enforces export controls and works with BIS.

Chips bound for companies headquartered in the US, or owned by a US parent, skip the licence as long as the exporter files the new reports. In practice that gives US-owned cloud providers in the region a faster path than local and Chinese-owned operators, which now queue for licences.

The rule also formally scraps the Biden administration's AI Diffusion Rule from January 2025, which sorted the world into three tiers and was never enforced. That includes its controls on model weights: the export class created for them, 4E091, is deleted.

Dates and duties for anyone in the GPU trade

The rule takes effect when it appears in the Federal Register. BIS posted it for public inspection on Monday, and Nixon Peabody expects formal publication on 19 November. Counting from that date:

  • About 19 December: a 30-day grace period ends for shipments that were already under way.
  • About 18 January: public comments close, 60 days after publication.
  • About 17 February: data centers already serving covered customers must hold a licence to keep doing it, or risk future export restrictions.

If you run or resell GPU capacity outside the US, start with your customer list. Map who ultimately owns each account, check whether your linked sites cross 12 million TPP, and document the checks, because the rule expects robust due diligence and treats a customer's refusal to answer as a warning sign. If you rent GPUs for your own work, expect longer onboarding questionnaires and ownership declarations from providers in Southeast Asia, and possibly tighter capacity there while licences are pending. If you are a Chinese-owned team using an offshore cloud, plan for that access to end in about three months.

Selling a chip and renting it out are now the same act in the eyes of US export law.

The bigger picture

For four years US chip policy chased boxes: where an accelerator was shipped and who signed for it. This rule moves the control point to the login, which is where AI compute is actually consumed. It will be hard to police, since "knowledge" of a customer's real owner is something BIS has to prove, and the cost lands hardest on the neutral cloud hubs that grew rich in between. Expect China to push harder on domestic accelerators like Huawei's Ascend, and expect the five-country list to grow, because the rule already gives the Secretary of Commerce the power to add more.
