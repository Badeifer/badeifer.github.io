The Dispatch

Nvidia's Jensen Huang is out after 33 years, and the CFO now runs the company

Nvidia's founder resigned on Thursday after a week of reports about his private life and a foreign intelligence probe. Here is what the board actually changed, what it promised customers, and what to watch before next Tuesday's earnings.

IndustryNovember 12, 20265 min read

Jensen Huang, who co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and ran it for all 33 years since, resigned as chief executive on Thursday, effective immediately. The company that sells most of the chips the AI industry runs on is now led on an interim basis by its finance chief, Colette Kress, with a board chair for the first time in its history.

For anyone who builds on Nvidia hardware or software, the short answer is that nothing you depend on changes this week. The longer answer is that the most founder-shaped company in tech just lost its founder in the middle of a congressional fight over chip exports, five days before earnings.

What the board actually did

Nvidia's press release and its Form 8-K, the filing US companies must make when a top officer leaves, line up on the facts. Huang resigned as president, CEO and director. The filing dates the resignation to Wednesday, 11 November, and the announcement came Thursday morning.

Kress, 59, has been chief financial officer since 2013. She keeps that job while serving as interim CEO until the board names a permanent one, and the board says it will look at internal and external candidates. Harvey Jones, 73, a director since 1993 and a former Synopsys chief executive, becomes Nvidia's first board chair. Until now Nvidia had no chair at all, an unusual setup for a company this size that left the founder with no one formally above him.

The 8-K is notable for what it leaves out. It discloses no severance, no separation agreement and no change to Huang's equity. A company spokesperson told NBC News that Huang will not work with Nvidia in any capacity going forward.

How it came apart in four days

The timeline is short, and Nvidia's own statements show how fast the ground moved.

The trigger was reporting by The New York Times and the Financial Times about a US government memo saying Huang had a romantic relationship with a business executive whom US intelligence considered an asset of China's spy service. According to NBC News, she held a senior post at Megaspeed, a Singapore company that bought advanced Nvidia chips and that, with related firms, is under US criminal investigation for allegedly smuggling them into China. China has called the allegations unsubstantiated. Nvidia says it follows US export law.

On Sunday the board opened an independent investigation, said it had no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised, and said it had confidence in Huang. Huang told staff in a memo that he had been unfaithful to his wife and supported the review. Then on Wednesday the Times, citing current and former employees, reported a second relationship, with an executive who reported to him. NBC said it had not verified that report. The board met that night. By Thursday morning he was gone.

None of the investigation's findings have been published, and Nvidia's Thursday release does not mention it at all. Treat any claim about what investigators concluded as unconfirmed until the company or a court says otherwise.

What the market did with it

Nvidia shares fell as much as 13% in the opening minutes on Thursday, according to both CNBC and Yahoo Finance, pushing its market value below $4 trillion before the stock pared some of the drop. The shares had closed Wednesday at $173.73, already well off their 6 October closing high of $239.24, and were heading for a sixth straight weekly decline, the longest run since 2012.

The money did not leave AI chips; it moved sideways. CNBC reported AMD up about 17% since Monday and Broadcom up more than 5% on the day, while the neoclouds that rent out Nvidia GPUs fell hard, with CoreWeave down about 12% and Nebius about 17%. Several analysts cut price targets, and one of them described a new "leadership discount" on the stock, in CNBC's account.

Nvidia's chips did not get worse on Thursday. Its answers to Washington just got harder to give.

What changes for people who buy and build on Nvidia

Very little, in the near term, and the board went out of its way to say so. Jones said in the release that he expects operations, customer commitments and the product roadmap to continue without interruption. CUDA, the drivers, the shipping GPUs and the parts already on order are the work of thousands of engineers, not one person in a leather jacket.

The things worth watching are slower-moving:

  • Tuesday's earnings. Nvidia still reports third-quarter fiscal 2027 results on 17 November, with figures around 1:20 pm Pacific and a call at 2 pm. Kress has fronted these calls for years as CFO; listen for whether guidance, supply commentary or the investigation change in tone.
  • Washington. Lawmakers want Huang to testify, and House Foreign Affairs chair Brian Mast says Huang is willing to do so as soon as next week. Senators have asked the administration to preserve records on Nvidia's China dealings. Tighter export rules or stricter customer checks would land on prices and lead times, not just on Nvidia.
  • The successor. Analysts quoted by CNBC have floated insiders such as Ian Buck, who leads Nvidia's hyperscale business, and chief scientist Bill Dally, while others expect an outside hire. An engineer from inside signals continuity. An outsider signals a reset.
  • Your own concentration. If your models, render farm or rented cluster assume Nvidia by default, this is a cheap moment to check how hard a move to AMD or another accelerator would be. You may never need it. Knowing the answer costs an afternoon.

The bigger picture

For a decade Nvidia's strategy and its founder were the same thing: the early bet on CUDA, the pivot to data centers, the yearly cadence of new chips. The company now has to prove that the machine he built keeps running without him, while its biggest regulatory question is about exactly the kind of China sales it can least afford to get wrong. The chips are fine. The next CEO inherits a political problem, not an engineering one.
