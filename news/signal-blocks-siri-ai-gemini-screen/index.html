The Dispatch

Signal now blinds Siri AI and Gemini to your chats, and admits the fix has holes

Signal turned screenshot blocking into a wall against the AI assistants built into phones. Here is what it covers on each platform, the settings to check today, and the routes it cannot close.

AINovember 23, 20265 min read

Signal has started blocking the AI assistants that ship inside iPhones and Android phones from reading your conversations. The tool it reached for is an old one, screenshot blocking, because on a modern phone a screenshot is exactly how Siri AI and Gemini "see" what is in another app. If you rely on Signal for anything sensitive, the end-to-end encryption still holds; the weak point has moved to the screen in your hand, and Signal is now saying so in public.

The announcement came on Friday, 20 November, in a post by Signal president Meredith Whittaker. It is short on drama and long on caveats. She calls the protection incomplete, lists the gaps herself and asks Apple, Google and Microsoft for tools that would make it unnecessary.

Why a screenshot is the attack

Encryption protects a message on the wire and on Signal's servers. It does nothing once the message is decrypted and drawn on your display, because by then it is just pixels. Agent-style assistants work by capturing those pixels. Siri AI's onscreen awareness, the feature that lets you ask Siri about whatever you are looking at, and Gemini's equivalent on Android both read the screen so they can act on it. Whatever they read can then be processed, summarised and in some cases sent off the device.

That makes the operating system a party to your chat that neither you nor your contact invited. Signal has been here before. In 2025 it switched on screen security by default in its Windows app after Microsoft's Recall feature started taking periodic snapshots of the desktop. The new move is the same idea carried to phones, where the assistants are more capable and more tightly woven into the system.

The weak point in an encrypted chat is no longer the network. It is the glass.

What changes on each platform

iPhone. On iOS 27, Signal now blocks screenshots, screen recordings and the preview you see in the App Switcher. The setting is called "Block Siri AI", and coverage from The Next Web and others reports it ships switched on. You can turn it off, and Signal will warn you that doing so may expose both your messages and your contacts' to AI agents.

Android. Signal does not block every screenshot here. Android comes in many vendor flavours, and Signal says it cannot reliably tell what is happening across all of them once screenshots are disabled. Instead it watches for an AI assistant trying to read the screen and, when it spots one, turns on its existing Screen security setting automatically.

Mac. Signal says it cannot block Siri AI on macOS 27 at all, because Apple offers no documented way to reliably stop screenshots there. The only fix is one you make yourself.

Windows. Nothing new. Screen security has been on by default on Windows 11 since the Recall episode.

Five minutes of settings worth doing

  1. Update Signal on every device you use it on, so the new behaviour is actually there.
  2. On an iPhone, open Signal's privacy settings and check that Block Siri AI is on. Then go to Settings, Siri, Excluded Apps and add Signal. Apple added that list in iOS 27.2, and the EFF reports that onscreen awareness simply returns an error for an excluded app. Belt and braces.
  3. On a Mac, add Signal to Excluded Apps under System Settings, Siri. This is the only protection available on macOS today.
  4. On Android, do not wait for detection. Turn on Screen security yourself in Signal's privacy settings, and review which apps hold accessibility permissions in the system settings.
  5. Talk to the people you message. Your words end up on their phone too. If a contact turns the block off, your half of the conversation is as exposed as theirs.

The trade-off is small but real: you lose the ability to screenshot your own chats. For most people that is a fair price.

The holes Signal names itself

Whittaker is blunt that this closes one door in a house with several. Screenshot blocking does nothing about an agent that reads files already stored on the device, such as attachments you saved. It does not cover Android phones where Signal cannot spot an assistant at work. And it does little against third-party agents that ask for broad permissions or accessibility access, which lets them read what is on screen through a different route. The post names Meta's Muse as an example; Reuters reported earlier in the week that Muse had pulled Signal messages on a Mac and on Android, and Meta says Muse only reaches other apps through permissions users grant.

Signal also waves off the industry's favourite reassurance. Processing assistant requests inside confidential computing enclaves, it says, is not a complete answer. The data has still left the app you chose to trust.

Whittaker called the problem "an existential threat to Signal". Her asks of the platform makers are concrete: a simple, documented way for any app to opt out of AI access to its screen, files, notifications and APIs without breaking itself; proper technical documentation of how their agents touch data; and limits on how third-party agents can use accessibility settings.

The bigger picture

For years the privacy fight was about the pipe between two phones, and the cryptographers largely won it. Assistants that sit on top of every app quietly reopen that fight at the endpoints, where an app developer has almost no say. Signal can afford to break a feature to make a point; most apps cannot. Whether Apple and Google ship a real opt-out will decide if "private app" still means anything on a phone with an agent built in, and every developer handling sensitive data should be asking them the same question.
