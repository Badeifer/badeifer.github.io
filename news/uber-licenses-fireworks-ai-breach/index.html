The Dispatch

Uber drivers' licences leaked through its AI vendor, and the vendor's story kept growing

Hackers used stolen Fireworks AI credentials to pull driver's licence images from Uber's data, while Fireworks spent six weeks widening what it admits was taken. Here is the timeline, what LLMjacking has to do with it, and what to check if your company sends data to an AI provider.

CyberNovember 16, 20265 min read

Uber is notifying US drivers that photos of their driver's licences, along with names, addresses, dates of birth and licence numbers, were stolen in October. Uber's own systems were not the way in. The attackers came through Fireworks AI, a cloud company that runs and fine-tunes open-source models for other businesses, using valid Fireworks credentials that let them read a storage area holding Uber data.

That makes this the clearest case yet of a problem many teams have quietly created for themselves: the AI vendor you send data to for a model job is now part of your attack surface, and it may hold more of your data, for longer, than anyone on your side remembers.

How a model host ended up holding licence photos

Uber's breach notice, filed with state attorneys general, says the attack happened on 7 October. The intruders used compromised credentials from a service provider to reach a cloud storage environment. Uber's lawyers told regulators the environment was watched by Uber's cloud security team and had alerting turned on, but nothing fired, because valid credentials and expected data flows look exactly like normal work.

Uber has not explained why licence images sat somewhere Fireworks could reach. Fireworks hosts inference and fine-tuning, so the likely answer is a document-processing or classification job, but neither company has said so, and it is worth treating as unknown.

The numbers are also incomplete. Uber has not published a national total. ITPro counted at least 16,236 affected drivers from filings in Texas, Iowa and South Carolina. TechTimes tallied 7,870 from Massachusetts, South Carolina and Rhode Island. California, New York and Illinois drivers have been notified without public counts, so the real figure is higher than any one tally.

Six weeks of "we now know more"

The Fireworks side of this is a lesson in how breach disclosures grow. Its first bulletin, posted on 9 October, said it spotted unauthorized use of internal credentials on 6 October. The attacker had read environment variables in evaluation jobs for a limited number of customer accounts, which Fireworks believed included API keys and tokens customers had stored as secrets. At that point it said it had found no evidence of access to customer models, datasets, training data, prompts or personal information.

Then on 23 October a third-party cloud provider hosting Fireworks research machines was itself breached. The next day an outsider used that foothold to run a workload on Fireworks development machines. Fireworks says it caught and stopped it the same day.

The 9 November update replaced the earlier reassurance. Attackers had reached resource metadata and billing details, including addresses, invoices, balances and credit usage, and for some customers, stored secrets, datasets and incomplete model weights. Card numbers were safe because Fireworks does not store them. Its inference service, training SDK and Nexus run with zero data retention, meaning prompts and outputs are not kept, and Fireworks says it found no impact there.

Notion was one of the customers. It says fewer than 1% of its workspaces were affected, but that the data included Notion AI chat transcripts used for its Insights feature, mostly without names or workspace IDs attached. Notion has switched Insights off and stopped serving open-weight models through Fireworks.

Why LLMjacking crews want an AI host

Fireworks says the evidence points to a financially motivated group that resells stolen access to language models through illicit routing gateways. The trade has a name, LLMjacking: steal the keys to someone's AI account, sell cheap tokens to strangers, and leave the victim to pay the compute bill. Google's threat intelligence team and Mandiant published research on these campaigns on 2 November.

An AI host is a rich target for that crowd. One break-in yields the host's own capacity plus whatever keys its customers parked there, which can include credentials for other model providers, cloud storage and databases. The Uber licences may have been a side effect of a group mostly hunting for compute, which is not much comfort to the drivers.

Your AI vendor is now a place your data lives, whether or not you meant it to.

What to check this week

If you use Fireworks, start with the notification email. Fireworks says it contacted every account it has found affected, and that no notice means no incident-specific action so far. Its October bulletin asked affected customers to do four things, and they are good practice for anyone who uses a hosted model platform:

  • Replace revoked Fireworks API keys and update the apps that use them.
  • Rotate any third-party credential you stored as a secret with the provider that issued it. Deleting the secret from Fireworks does not kill the credential.
  • Check the providers behind those credentials for reads, downloads or changes you did not make, and rotate even if the logs look clean.
  • Look for resources you did not create, such as new API keys, users, training jobs or datasets.

Everyone else should run a smaller audit. List the AI vendors that hold your datasets, fine-tuning files or evaluation secrets, and delete what no running job needs. Give evaluation jobs short-lived, narrow keys rather than production ones. Put a spend alert on every model API account, since a sudden bill is often the first sign of LLMjacking. And ask each vendor which of its services keep data and which do not, because "zero retention" in Fireworks' case covered inference and not the datasets that leaked.

If you drive for Uber in the US, read the notice carefully for any credit monitoring offer and its enrolment deadline, and consider a credit freeze, since a licence image plus a date of birth is most of what identity fraud needs.

The bigger picture

Security teams spent a decade learning to inventory their SaaS vendors. AI providers slipped in through a side door, usually as a developer's experiment with a company card, and they collect exactly the material attackers prize: raw documents, labelled datasets and keys to other systems. The Fireworks case will not be the last disclosure that grows in stages. The useful response is to know, before the next bulletin, what you have handed to each of these companies.
