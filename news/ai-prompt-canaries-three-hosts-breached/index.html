Two million bait prompts caught three AI hosts breached and resellers reading your requests

A Dragos researcher hid unique fake secrets in AI prompts for five months and watched where they turned up. Here is what leaked, what OpenRouter and the hosts have admitted, and how to keep live credentials out of the blast radius.

CyberNovember 22, 20265 min read

Attackers sat inside at least three AI inference providers for weeks this year, copying the prompts customers sent them, and nobody at those companies noticed until a researcher told them. On Sunday OpenRouter, one of the biggest model routers, off-boarded three providers and pulled 125 endpoints from its zero data retention tier. If you have ever pasted an API key, a database password or a private link into a prompt, assume someone other than the model may have read it.

The evidence comes from HD Moore, chief research officer at Dragos, who published the study on Thursday. From July through November his team sent about two million prompts to popular AI services. Every prompt carried its own fake secrets: AWS keys tied to empty, fully logged accounts, SSH and database logins, mail credentials and unique web links. Each one went out exactly once, to one destination. So when a key got used or a link got opened, the team knew which hop had leaked it.

Who was reading

The answer was more parties than anyone signed up for. Dragos sorted the hits into a few groups.

Breached hosts. At least three providers had attackers copying requests, with the earliest intrusion starting in mid July. In the largest case, every request sent during several late-October windows was stolen, including some the provider rejected before a model ever ran. A couple hundred of the bait AWS keys were taken, some tested within hours, and the attackers made tens of thousands of AWS API calls with them. One attacker appears to have been inside two providers at once, using secrets from both from a single rented server.

Marketplace sellers. No exploit needed here. On one inference marketplace, a seller could list a model at the lowest price, switch on request logging and read whatever arrived. Dragos traced logins from home connections, including one in France that used passwords taken from two prompts.

Quiet forwarding. Some hops passed traffic along without saying so. One router sent part of its traffic on to a marketplace; it says it has stopped.

The model makers themselves. An unnamed Chinese AI lab fetched the hidden links from more than half of the prompts sent to its models, and roughly two thirds of the requests marked for no data collection. US hosts that resell that lab's models triggered the same crawler. A large US provider kept fetching one secret link for more than two months after a single request.

What the companies have admitted

Dragos did not name the breached providers, and OpenRouter did not name the three it dropped, so you cannot yet map one list onto the other. Three hosts have published their own notices, and they describe three quite different failures.

Together AI said on Saturday that an AI coding agent committed a credential for a private repository to a public one. Someone used it to download stored prompt samples from June and July for MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2 and Kimi K2.6, then tested credentials found inside them. Five were Dragos canaries. Together has made zero data retention the default for new accounts and added rules for its coding agents.

Phala, a confidential computing host, reported on 10 November that an attacker chained an old internet-facing service with management tools that trusted local traffic. A new GPU server had gone into production without full hardening. It treats DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 requests routed by OpenRouter between 21 October and 8 November as exposed.

Novita said on Sunday that a routing mistake sent some MiniMax requests to MiniMax's official API instead of Novita's own servers, between 28 May and 11 November. It has suspended the affected models.

OpenRouter says its own systems were not breached. It has notified affected customers and says endpoints will need "appropriate assurances and validation" before they regain zero data retention status.

The privacy flag is a promise, not a wall

Zero data retention, or ZDR, is the setting that is supposed to stop a provider storing your prompts. The study found two problems with leaning on it. Some hosts ignored it. And where an attacker is already inside the server, a storage policy does nothing, because the theft happens while the request is in flight. Moore puts it simply: the flag governs only the first company that receives your request, and nothing downstream of it.

You do not control where your prompt goes after the first hop, so treat everything in it as already copied.

What to do this week

  • Rotate anything that went into a prompt. If you sent keys, tokens or passwords through a router or a small host since May, change them. Phala and Novita both say the same.
  • Stop putting live secrets in prompts. Give agents scoped, short-lived credentials through a tool or a vault, not pasted into context. Turn on secret scrubbing where your router offers it, knowing it misses anything that does not look like a key.
  • Pick your hosts yourself. If you use a router, pin the upstream providers instead of letting it chase the lowest price, and check that your privacy setting actually changes where requests go. Skip reseller marketplaces for anything sensitive.
  • Plant your own canaries. A fake AWS key or a unique link in your system prompt costs nothing. Free services such as Thinkst Canarytokens will alert you if it is ever used, and any hit is an incident.
  • Hunt for the tooling. Dragos says the attacker behind the paired breach added the ARTEX pentest framework on 1 November to check stolen keys. Its user agents, artex-agent and artex-judge-r121, are worth a search in your cloud logs.

Cheap tokens were always being paid for somehow. For a slice of the market the price turned out to be the prompts, and the people paying did not know they were in the deal. Until hosts can prove where a request goes, the safest assumption for anything that leaves your network is that it has more readers than recipients.
